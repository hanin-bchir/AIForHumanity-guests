The Mission

AI for Everyday Health

An ideathon where students of all levels explore how AI can help real people notice a health problem before it becomes serious — in a simple, accessible, and human-centered way.

◈
Open to All Levels
No technical experience required. Beginners focus on the human problem. Advanced students go deeper into how the AI works. Both levels compete equally under the same criteria.
◉
Real-World Impact
Every idea must address a genuine health challenge in Tunisia or the MENA region — grounded in reality. Feasibility is a core judging criterion.
◎
Industry Mentorship
Two CEOs from the health-tech and AI-engineering space will mentor every team, offering direct feedback drawn from real Tunisian startup experience.
⬡
SIGHT Aligned
This event is built on the IEEE SIGHT mission — using engineering as a force for human good. Every idea pitched today is a step toward accessible, intelligent healthcare.

"How can AI help someone notice a health problem before it becomes serious — in a simple, accessible way?"

01
Detect
AI that spots a health signal before the person knows something is wrong. Early warning. Silent monitoring. Prevention.
02
Guide
AI that helps someone manage an existing condition — reminders, coaching, personalized advice, behavior change.
03
Access
AI that brings healthcare to people who can't easily reach it — rural areas, cost barriers, language, disability.
Who We Are

About IEEE

The world's largest technical professional organization — and the community behind this event.

IEEE — the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers — is the world's largest technical professional organization, dedicated to advancing technology for the benefit of humanity.

Founded in 1963, IEEE brings together engineers, scientists, and professionals from over 160 countries. It publishes more than a third of the world's technical literature in electrical engineering, electronics, and computing, and organizes thousands of conferences and events globally each year.

IEEE SIGHT (Special Interest Group on Humanitarian Technology) is the IEEE branch dedicated to using engineering and technology to address humanitarian challenges — bridging the gap between technical innovation and real-world social impact.

IEEE SB ESPRIM is the Student Branch of IEEE at ESPRIM, Monastir. We organize technical workshops, hackathons, competitions, and community events to connect students with the global IEEE network and inspire a generation of engineers who build for good.

Your Compass

The Ideation Canvas

Every team works through this 3-step framework during the workshop. Simple enough for first-year students. Deep enough for final-year engineers.

01
The Problem
Who suffers? What do they miss? Why does the current system fail them? Describe a real person in a real situation in Tunisia or the MENA region.
02
The AI Role
What does the AI detect, predict, or suggest? In plain language. What data does it use? What decision does it make for the user?
03
The Impact
Who benefits and how? How does their daily life concretely change? Is this realistic to build and deploy? Who would use it?

The Verdict

Judging & Scoring

Every pitch is evaluated on five criteria by a panel of three independent jurors. Here is exactly how scoring works and how winners are decided.

Each team has 3 minutes to pitch followed by 2 minutes of jury Q&A. Each juror scores independently on a private scoresheet. Scores are averaged only after all pitches are complete — not after each one — to prevent early bias. Total possible score: 100 points.

25
points
Impact
Does this solve a real, significant health problem? Who benefits and how many people? Is the social impact measurable and meaningful for the MENA region?
20
points
AI Relevance
Is AI genuinely necessary, or could a simple app do the same? Is the AI role clearly explained? Beginners judged on clarity of concept, not technical depth.
20
points
Feasibility
Could this realistically exist? Not "can you build it today" but "is the idea grounded in reality?" Are technical and clinical constraints understood?
20
points
Innovation
Is the angle fresh? Does it combine domains unexpectedly? Does it solve a health problem others have overlooked or approached differently?
15
points
Pitch Clarity
Is the problem clear in the first 30 seconds? Can a non-technical person understand the solution? Is the 3 minutes well structured?
◈ During the Pitch Session
01
Each team presents for exactly 3 minutes. A visible countdown timer is shown to the presenter at all times.
02
The jury asks questions for 2 minutes. Questions focus on the weakest or most interesting part of the idea.
03
Each juror fills their private scoresheet immediately after each team — with zero discussion between jurors until all pitches are done.
04
No scores are revealed during the session. Teams do not know their standing until the deliberation is complete.
◉ Juror Responsibilities
AC
Abdelhamid Chaoua leads on Impact — evaluates whether the health problem is real, the patient journey is understood, and the outcome is clinically meaningful.
KM
Kabil Mbarki leads on AI Relevance & Feasibility — evaluates whether the AI mechanism is sound and whether the solution could realistically be built and deployed.
OR
Organizer juror leads on Clarity & Innovation — evaluates communication quality and the originality of the idea's angle.
⚠
All three jurors score all five criteria independently. Lead roles apply only during deliberation discussion, not to scoring exclusivity.
How the Final Decision is Made — Step by Step
01
All pitches done
Jury closes the pitch session. Participants wait while jury enters deliberation.
→
02
Scores compiled
Organizer collects all 3 scoresheets. Calculates average score per team across all jurors.
→
03
Ranking built
Teams ranked by total average out of 100. Top 3 identified. Any ties are noted.
→
04
Tie-breaking
Equal scores? Jury discusses and breaks ties using Impact first, then AI Relevance.
→
05
Winners confirmed
All 3 jurors agree on the final ranking before returning. Results announced with brief feedback.
A brilliant idea clearly explained by a first-year student should score higher than a complex system poorly communicated by a final-year one. Clarity and human impact always win over technical complexity.
